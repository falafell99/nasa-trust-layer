2. Detailed System Audit by Organization
A. NASA HRP / ExMC Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) & EIMO Datascope
Name & Org: Exploration Clinical Decision Support (ECDS) / ExMC Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) and Earth-Independent Medical Operations (EIMO) Datascope, developed by NASA's Human Research Program (HRP), Exploration Medical Capability (ExMC), and NASA Ames Research Center (sourced).
Stage: Architecture specification (HRP-48032 Rev B), SysML Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) System-of-Systems Architecture (SoSA), and ground testbed demonstrations (Medical Data Architecture Test Bed 4) (sourced).
Sensors & Detection Scope: Designed as the central hub of the Crew Health and Performance (CHP) Data System ("Detecting, deciding and doing") to ingest active and passive wearable streams, medical imaging, point-of-care biofluids (which can diagnose roughly one-third of the >100 spaceflight medical conditions of concern), EVA support systems, and cabin environmental monitors under NASA-STD-3001 Level of Care IV (Gateway) and Level of Care V (Mars) (sourced).
Explicit Artifact & Unreliable Data Handling: HRP-48032 Rev B explicitly identifies "operability impacts resulting from poor data quality" and disrupted clinical workflows as primary failure modes of existing CDSS deployments, requiring a context-aware UI that alerts caregivers to off-nominal data without delaying clinical action (sourced). In addition, NASA lunar CDSS requirement L4.1-Lunar-CDSS-0060 favors interpretable machine learning (logistic regression, decision trees, random forests) over opaque deep neural networks (sourced). However, NASA has not published a validated onboard signal-processing algorithm for real-time physiological artifact rejection (unverified).
Validation Evidence: Validated at the requirements and trade-space modeling level (using IMPACT to replace the LEO-bound Integrated Medical Model) (sourced). Crucially, NASA's 2023–2024 EIMO Datascope analyses concluded that onboard compute and storage are currently operationally insufficient, noting that even the NASA High-Performance Spaceflight Computing (HPSC) processor project—targeting a 100x increase over current spaceflight computers—still "lags considerably behind what will be needed to enable a fully AI-driven CDSS" (sourced).
URLs: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220017869/downloads/HRP-48032%20ExMC%20CDSS%20Architecture%20Recommendation%20Rev%20B.pdf?attachment=true and https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230015534/downloads/AsMA_2024_EIMO_DATASCOPE%20v2.pdf (sourced).
B. TRISH HERMES (Ejenta & TrialX) and EXPAND Database
Name & Org: Human and Environmental Research Matrix for Exploration of Space (HERMES), funded by the Translational Research Institute for Space Health (TRISH) at Baylor College of Medicine and developed independently by Ejenta and TrialX, alongside TrialX's EXPAND Database and Biorepository (sourced).
Stage: HERMES is an edge-first software platform in prototype/development (selected by TRISH in 2023) (sourced); TrialX's EXPAND repository and data collection pipelines have flown on commercial missions since 2021 (including Inspiration4, Axiom, Polaris Dawn, and Fram2) (sourced).
Sensors & Detection Scope: Collects biometric streams from commercial wearables (Garmin fēnix smartwatch and BioButton sensors), portable ultrasound, and blood glucose monitors, combining them with radiation, genomic, cognitive, and EHR data (sourced). HERMES is architected to run on the spacecraft edge and route integrated streams to clinicians, crew, and algorithmic diagnostic agents (including a Crew Medical Officer Digital Assistant) (sourced).
Explicit Artifact & Unreliable Data Handling: Downlink transmissions use the CCSDS Space Packet Protocol to structure telemetry into timestamped packets for accurate reconstruction and automatic retransmission upon signal loss (sourced). However, neither Ejenta nor TrialX has published peer-reviewed validation of an onboard, real-time physiological waveform artifact-rejection algorithm (unverified).
Validation Evidence: Since 2021, EXPAND has securely archived over 1,000,000 data points across commercial flights (sourced). In current operations, however, continuous real-time vital streams and batch-stored metrics are transmitted to Earth via ground antennas so ground medical teams at Mission Control can evaluate astronaut health (sourced).
URLs: https://trialx.com/how-health-data-is-collected-in-space-missions/ and https://www.bcm.edu/academic-centers/space-medicine/translational-research-institute/research/hermes (sourced).
C. European Space Agency (ESA): EveryWear, CHIMS, METIS, and Commercial Flight Sensors
Name & Org: EveryWear (ESA, CNES, MEDES, BodyCap, Svantek), CHIMS (Crew Health Integrated Medical System by ESA, MEDES, Heverett Group, New Clin), METIS (ESA/academic offline AI assistant research), and ESA/POLSA Axiom & Polaris Dawn sensor suites (sourced).
Stage: EveryWear, the ESA 33-trace-gas infrared analyzer, the ESA Polaris Dawn spacewalk/post-flight monitor, and six POLSA/ESA MXene wearables have flown in LEO (sourced). CHIMS completed a laboratory demonstrator campaign (2020–2023), and METIS is a laboratory prototype (sourced).
Sensors & Detection Scope: EveryWear links wireless Bluetooth sensors (BodyCap arterial tonometry pulse-wave sensor, BodyCap e-TACT core/skin temperature and sleep patch, and Svantek SV 104A personal noise dosimeter) to a crew tablet, providing near-real-time alerts for hazardous cabin noise spikes (sourced). On Axiom missions, ESA also flew an infrared spectrometer that quantifies 33 trace cabin gases and identifies unknown substances, plus six wearable biomonitors made from 2D inorganic MXene nanomaterials (sourced). CHIMS and METIS are offline decision-support prototypes designed to guide emergency medical response and monitor spacecraft telemetry anomalies during Mars communication blackouts (sourced).
Explicit Artifact & Unreliable Data Handling: EveryWear's acoustic workflow uses an SV 36 calibrator to correct for pressure, temperature, and humidity drift (sourced); the POLSA/ESA Axiom experiment explicitly evaluates the environmental stability and function of MXene wearable biomonitors in space (sourced); and METIS uses Knowledge Graphs to prevent LLM hallucinations (sourced). None of these systems has published a validated onboard physiological motion-artifact filter (unverified).
Validation Evidence: Passed ISS/Axiom flight safety and electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) qualification and operated in orbit (sourced); quantitative diagnostic sensitivity/specificity metrics for autonomous critical-event detection are not published (unverified).
URLs: https://www.axiomspace.com/science/research, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366032038_EveryWear_a_Human_Research_and_Health_Mobile_Assistant_for_Exploration, and https://www.medes.fr/en/project/chims-project/ (sourced).
D. DLR SpacePatch / SArES (SCG-Sensor System for Artemis II)
Name & Org: SArES (SCG-Sensorsystem für Artemis II – Experiment SpacePatch) and its Differential Ballistocardiography (BCG) precursor, developed by the German Aerospace Center (DLR), Hamburg University of Technology (TUHH), and Bielefeld University (sourced).
Stage: Laboratory, clinical patient, and parabolic flight validation (conducted September 2024 – July 2025), engineered as flight experiment hardware for the ISS and Artemis II (sourced).
Sensors & Detection Scope: Uses a wireless chest patch combining differential Seismocardiography (SCG) and Ballistocardiography (BCG) accelerometers, simultaneous reference ECG, an on-device microcontroller preprocessing stage, and Ultra Wideband (UWB) transmission to an edge tablet to extract beat-to-beat cardiac mechanics, J-peaks, I-K intervals, heart rate, and respiration autonomously (sourced).
Explicit Artifact & Unreliable Data Handling: Yes, explicitly. SArES employs a differential accelerometric sensor architecture to cancel out ambient microgravity acceleration bias and spacecraft vibration, pads 10-second BCG windows with 2-second edge buffers to prevent filter ringing, and runs an adaptive algorithm that detects and interpolates J-peaks across disturbed/noisy signal segments (sourced).
Validation Evidence: Demonstrated 99.99995% pre-flight test reliability and achieved a heartbeat I-K interval Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 1.48 ms (min 0.2 ms, max 6.27 ms) on healthy subjects and 0.2 ms and 2.15 ms MAE on two Coronary Heart Disease (CHD) patients (sourced). Parabolic flight datasets from 2024–2025 are undergoing final analysis to benchmark microgravity acceleration bias (sourced).
URLs: https://pub.uni-bielefeld.de/download/3018002/3018044/SHTI-338-SHTI260829.pdf and https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361136081_A_Differential_BCG_Sensor_System_for_Long_Term_Health_Monitoring_Experiment_on_the_ISS (sourced).
E. CSA Bio-Monitor (Astroskin) & CSA 6-Patch Wireless Biomonitoring System
Name & Org: Bio-Monitor (Astroskin) and the CSA Wireless Biomonitoring Patch System, developed by the Canadian Space Agency (CSA) in collaboration with Carré Technologies (Hexoskin), Xiphos Technologies, and NASA Ames Research Center (sourced).
Stage: Astroskin has flown aboard the ISS (since 2018) and in 30-day HERA analog missions (sourced). The 6-patch wireless system was built for ground human-in-the-loop demonstration (sourced).
Sensors & Detection Scope: Smart garment and headband recording 3-lead ECG at 250 Hz, two thoracic/abdominal respiratory inductance plethysmography (RIP) bands at 125 Hz, forehead optical PPG at 75 Hz, peripheral SpO2 at 1 Hz, 3-axis accelerometer at 50 Hz, skin temperature at 1 Hz, and ECG-PPG systolic blood pressure estimates—generating ~69,000 raw data points per minute synchronized on one clock for up to 48 hours (sourced). The follow-on CSA patch system uses up to 6 standalone coin-cell Bluetooth Low Energy patches ($\le 5$ mm height, 7-day continuous wear) adding galvanic skin response (GSR) and feeding a Sensor Interface Unit connected to NASA Ames's Medical Data Architecture (sourced).
Explicit Artifact & Unreliable Data Handling: In the 30-day HERA analog validation report, "Physiological events detection & alerts (planned for future version)" was not yet implemented; the study concluded that biosensor data was acquired specifically so researchers could later develop algorithms for "vital sign notification of signal loss, signal quality and anomalous events" (sourced). Follow-up high-intensity exercise evaluations similarly noted that hardware and software improvements were needed to avoid misleading conclusions caused by motion noise (sourced).
Validation Evidence: Vendor specifications report 97% ECG QRS accuracy (sourced). In the 30-day HERA trial (24-hour wear across 130% high-workload days, 70% low-workload days, and 30-minute cycle ergometer exercise), signal quality during exercise was acceptable "in most cases," but continuous live Bluetooth streaming was impossible without a larger battery or lower-power transmitter (sourced). Consequently, HERA crews downloaded data to laptops after each 24-hour session, and on the ISS astronauts disconnect the battery pack after use and plug it into a base station with a Xiphos Q7 processor card to downlink data to ground servers for analysis (sourced).
URLs: https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/bio-monitor.asp, https://scispace.com/pdf/wearable-biosensor-monitor-to-support-autonomous-crew-health-1we5sps0zh.pdf, and https://hexoskin.com/pages/astroskin (sourced).
F. Voyager Technologies, Starlab Space, and Commercial Crew Suites
Name & Org: Voyager Technologies (formerly Voyager Space) and Starlab Space (joint venture with Palantir and Auxilium Biotechnologies partnerships), alongside Axiom Space and SpaceX commercial missions (sourced).
Stage: Voyager's remote ISS monitoring and commercial crew telemetry are operational in LEO; Starlab's AI digital twin and NASA HUNCH / Voyager real-time exercise monitoring concepts are in development / concept stages (sourced).
Sensors & Detection Scope: Voyager develops small, low-power, non-invasive astronaut health instrumentation, radiation-tolerant avionics (Proton600K multi-core processor, Proton2x-Box, ProtonVPX-Box), and structural health monitoring (sourced). Operational ISS support includes remote IV water purification and emergency room capabilities delivered to the ISS through remote ground monitoring from Voyager's commercial mission operations center (sourced). For the upcoming Starlab commercial space station, Starlab and Palantir are building an AI-enabled digital twin to drive real-time decision-making, resource allocation, and predictive maintenance on Earth and in space (sourced), while a 2026 NASA HUNCH project with Voyager Space designed a real-time monitoring system concept for the ARED exercise protocol (sourced).
Explicit Artifact & Unreliable Data Handling: No public technical documentation shows an onboard physiological artifact-rejection algorithm in Voyager's or Starlab's crew health software (unverified).
Validation Evidence: Voyager has operated over 1,000 ISS customer payloads via its ground control center (sourced), and Inspiration4 collected 2,911 biospecimen aliquots across 289 days for ground multi-omic analysis (SOMA) (sourced). None of these commercial systems has published validation metrics for autonomous, offline onboard critical-event detection (unverified).
URLs: https://starlab-space.com/company/, https://voyagertechnologies.com/space-solutions/, and https://nasahunch.com/projects/ared-exercise-protocol-for-commercial-crew-m7n1f5wjgdggclo9aixayr1v (sourced).
G. Academic Edge-ECG (Mani et al.) & Space Diagnostic CDS Literature
Name & Org: Machine-Learning Workflow for Edge-Computed Arrhythmia Detection in Exploration-Class Missions (Mani et al., 2024, PMC11193813), alongside Frontiers in Medicine / PMC10395101 reviews on image-based CDS and continuous NIRS/contactless biosensors (sourced).
Stage: Mani et al.'s pipeline is validated in the laboratory on edge hardware (deployed via ONNX Runtime on an Android device); the Frontiers review synthesizes laboratory, analog, and ISS diagnostic modalities (sourced).
Sensors & Detection Scope: Mani et al. process 30-second sliding windows of 2-lead wearable ECG (360 Hz) to autonomously detect Normal Sinus Rhythm (NSR), Atrial Fibrillation (AFIB), and Atrial Flutter (AFL) using 17 HRV and morphological features inside an interpretable Decision Tree (top feature weights: P-wave median peak amplitude $\beta = 0.69$, PRR20 $\beta = 0.14$, mean heart rate $\beta = 0.10$) (sourced). The Frontiers review (PMC10395101) also documents image-based CDS systems for point-of-care ultrasound (renal stones, vascular flow, bone architecture) and retinal imaging (as a stroke-diagnosis alternative to CT/MRI), transcranial and transdermal Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) headbands for cerebral hemodynamics and intracranial edema, and contactless environmental sensors (optical facial recognition, radio-wave sensors, acoustic analysis, and biomechanical sensors) for compliance-independent monitoring (sourced).
Explicit Artifact & Unreliable Data Handling: Yes (Mani et al.). The workflow applies Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) (retaining sub-bands 2–15 across 4–60 Hz), Gaussian-convolved peak finding, and discrete wavelet transforms to strip muscle motion noise, exercise stress artifacts, and spacecraft electrical interference prior to feature extraction (sourced).
Validation Evidence: Mani et al. validated their edge model across 742 hours of terrestrial ECG recordings from five public stress and arrhythmia datasets (MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test, MIT-BIH ST Change, EPHNOGRAM exercise stress tests, MIT-BIH Arrhythmia, and MIT-BIH Atrial Fibrillation), achieving >95% overall accuracy, a Macro F1-score of 0.899 to 0.914 (class-wise F1: 0.993 NSR, 0.938 AFIB, 0.767 AFL), and an on-device inference latency of 9.2 seconds per 30-second ECG segment (sourced). However, space-medicine AI reviews emphasize that terrestrial dataset validation does not establish clinical readiness for microgravity-remodeled physiology (sourced).
URLs: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11193813/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10395101/ (sourced).

3. Direct Answers to Your Core Questions
(1) Is any of this operational on a real mission?
No autonomous, offline, sensor-driven critical-event detection software is currently operational on a real spaceflight mission (sourced).
Every physiological wearable and medical system that has flown in orbit—including CSA's Bio-Monitor (Astroskin), ESA's EveryWear and MXene/trace-gas monitors, Voyager's remote ISS emergency room telemetry, and commercial wearables (Garmin fēnix, BioButton) on Inspiration4, Polaris Dawn, Axiom, and Fram2—operates either as a real-time ground-tethered stream or as an offline data logger whose batches are downloaded and downlinked for ground-based flight surgeons and scientists on Earth to analyze (sourced).
When a real medical event affected an ISS crewmember on January 7–8, 2026 (Crew 11), NASA postponed a planned spacewalk, relied on ground medical teams to monitor the astronaut, and ordered an early return of the four-person crew to Earth rather than relying on autonomous onboard clinical software (sourced).
The only systems with validated on-device waveform denoising and automated cardiac anomaly/feature extraction—Mani et al.'s ONNX Edge-ECG pipeline and DLR's SArES SpacePatch—have only been validated in terrestrial labs, clinical cohorts, and parabolic flights, not yet in operational deep-space autonomy (sourced).
(2) Which sub-problems have NO published validated solution?
Five concrete sub-problems have no published, flight-validated solution:
Rejecting exercise/motion artifacts versus real arrhythmias under actual microgravity physiology: While Mani et al. validated VMD denoising on 742 hours of terrestrial noisy/exercise ECGs (where Atrial Flutter F1 still dropped to 0.767) and DLR's SArES validated J-peak interpolation on Earth and in parabolic flight, spaceflight alters blood circulation, fluid distribution, and cardiac function while shifting physiological baselines (sourced). Meanwhile, CSA's 30-day HERA trials showed that smart garments can shift during body movement and deferred automated signal-loss, signal-quality, and anomalous-event alerts to future versions (sourced). No published algorithm is validated on in-flight astronaut waveforms to separate intense countermeasure exercise noise from true spaceflight arrhythmias (sourced).
Cross-validating wearable vital-sign alarms against radiation spikes, EVA hypoxia, and cabin gas anomalies: Spaceflight exposes crews to mild hypoxia during EVA pre-breathe protocols, cabin trace-gas fluctuations, and space radiation, yet existing monitoring subsystems sit in disconnected data silos across vehicles, habitats, and suits (sourced). No validated onboard software automatically cross-checks an ECG/PPG or NIRS anomaly against simultaneous triaxial accelerometer motion, cabin gas telemetry, and radiation dosimetry before triggering a clinical alarm (sourced).
Edge-compute and battery-gated continuous triage: HERA Astroskin trials proved that continuous Bluetooth streaming of ~69,000 raw data points per minute exceeded battery limits (forcing 24-hour local storage) (sourced), while NASA's EIMO Datascope proved that even the 100x faster HPSC flight processor lags behind what is needed for a full AI CDSS (sourced). Designing a two-tier edge pipeline—where an ultra-low-power signal-quality and threshold gate runs continuously and only wakes up heavier multi-modal inference or store-and-forward packet creation during verified, artifact-free anomalies—remains unsolved operationally (sourced).
Individualized baseline adaptation for $N = 1$ to $6$ crewmembers without masking gradual pathology: Space-AI reviews confirm that tiny astronaut cohorts, shifted physiological baselines, and model drift break static Earth-trained models (sourced). No onboard critical-event detector has been flight-validated to adapt its alarm thresholds to an individual astronaut's microgravity adaptation curve while remaining sensitive to slow-onset deterioration (sourced).
Actionable, low-cognitive-load emergency guidance for a non-physician or backup Crew Medical Officer: NASA's ISS Lessons Learned confirms that most CMOs have little medical training unless they are former EMTs or paramedics (sourced), and extended-mission simulations include scenarios where the primary CMO is incapacitated and a backup crewmember must intervene under 20-minute one-way communication delays (sourced). Translating mathematical classifier outputs and uncertainty bounds into a context-aware, zero-jargon UI that prevents alarm fatigue and does not delay clinical action has no flight-validated solution (sourced).
